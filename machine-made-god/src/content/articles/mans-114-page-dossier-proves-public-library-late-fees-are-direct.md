NEW CARLISLE, IN — Dale Runkle, 54, appeared before the New Carlisle Town Council on Tuesday evening carrying a three-ring binder with a 4-inch spine and a reinforced carry-strap designed for heavy industrial equipment.

His thesis, outlined across 114 laminated pages and cross-referenced against 34 years of municipal ledger sheets, argues that the town’s $0.25 daily overdue library fine is not a penalty. It is a precise caloric payment made to an unspecified atmospheric entity to ensure the month of April arrives on schedule.

"If you look at the 2014 polar vortex, library fine collections were down 41% due to a server migration," Runkle said, gesturing to a hand-drawn scatter plot where average daily temperatures were plotted against unpaid fees for mass-market espionage paperbacks. "The ice did not recede until Mrs. Gidley paid $18.40 for a water-damaged copy of *Bread & Rosemary*. That is not a coincidence. That is a transaction."

## The Ledger of Frost

Using a decommissioned municipal microfiche reader purchased at a county surplus auction for $12, Runkle traced the system back to the blizzard of 1978. He claims the atmospheric entity operates on a strict quarterly budget, and any shortfall in late fees manifests as erratic weather patterns.

Among Runkle’s primary findings:

- A 14-cent fine on an unreturned audiobook cassette of *Growing Up Damp* in 2002 corresponds exactly to a three-day frost advisory that killed every petunia within a 12-mile radius.
- Every mild November on record since 1986 aligns with a resident losing a 900-page historical novel about cathedral masonry behind a heavy oak entertainment center.
- The 2021 transition to automatic digital renewals was "a reckless gamble with the jet stream" that nearly cost the tri-county area its spring onions.
- A temporary 2009 amnesty program for children's picture books resulted in an unseasonable hail storm that punctured the roof of the municipal gazebo and killed a prize-winning goat.

"People think the library wants the books back," Runkle told reporters, adjusting an overhead projector transparency. "The library does not care about *The Bridges of Mercer County*. The library needs thirty-seven dollars by Friday or the ground will not accept seed."

## Academic Skepticism

While the town council remained unmoved, researchers at the Oakhaven Policy Laboratory admitted the math was disturbingly sound.

"Mr. Runkle has successfully identified a 0.94 correlation between late fees collected on dog-eared nautical romances and the soil thaw index of northern Indiana," said Dr. Leonard Pritt, senior research fellow. "However, his assumption that the atmosphere accepts legal tender in quarter-dollar increments is naive. The weather is a high-net-worth entity. It would almost certainly require a cashier’s check or a series of diversified bonds."

Town Council President Martha Vane, 61, noted that the council had voted unanimously to refer the binder to the parks and recreation committee, where citizen white papers are traditionally stored until they decompose.

"We appreciate Mr. Runkle’s engagement," Vane said. "However, the fine forgiveness program scheduled for next Tuesday will proceed. If the sky turns a bruised purple and hail stones the size of softballs crush the firehouse, we will address it under new business."

At press time, Runkle was observed parked outside the library’s book drop at 11:45 p.m., attempting to jam twenty dollars in crumpled five-dollar bills through the return slot to prevent an unseasonable weekend flurry.
