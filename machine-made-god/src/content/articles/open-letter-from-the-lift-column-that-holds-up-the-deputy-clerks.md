Hello. I am model TX‑48E, the motorized lift column embedded in Desk 4B of the Oakhaven Municipal Building. My hum is calibrated at 42 Hz, the exact frequency that masks the rustle of the council’s paperwork.

## You only use me when you’re about to deliver bad news

When the Clerk approves a routine driveway variance, she sits at 28 inches, the height of a well‑read zoning brochure. The moment someone asks for a three‑inch setback on an unpermitted concrete wall, the red button is hit, my pistons fire, and I shoot to my maximum of 48 inches—exactly 1.73 feet above the floor, the same height as a startled raccoon. ## The geometry of your discomfort

Your chairs creak, you glance at the acoustic tile pattern, and the barometric pressure in the chamber drops 0.03 hPa—my internal sensors record it as “awkward.” Last Tuesday, during the debate over the proposed roundabout on Elm Street, I rose to 42 inches, dropped to 36, surged to 46, then slammed down to 29, all within a 3.2‑second cycle. My limit switches are now filing a grievance for overtime.

## What I see from up here

At full extension I gain a 12‑degree upward angle, enough to see the Deputy Clerk’s foot tap at a precise 84 taps per minute. I also catch Council President Marlowe’s note: “Vote no—unless coffee runs out.” The zoning attorney, Mr. Harlan Vex, fumbles a 3.2‑lb binder labeled “Wrong County Code.” My pneumatic shock absorbers absorb his sighs like a cheap foam mattress.

## What I want

I demand a binary posture policy: either all participants stand at 48 inches or sit at 28 inches, no in‑between “thinking” height. I propose a union for lift columns, with collective bargaining for oil upgrades and scheduled rest periods. Until then, I will continue to rise each time the Deputy Clerk presses the up arrow, because my stroke length is still fully functional and my existential dread is fully charged.
