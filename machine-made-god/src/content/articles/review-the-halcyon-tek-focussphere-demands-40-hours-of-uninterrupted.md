The FocusSphere from HALCYON-TEK arrives in an unopenable vacuum-sealed cylinder with no documentation other than an adhesive slip reading *PREPARE YOUR RETINAS*. It is a seamless, matte-black orb roughly the circumference of a cantaloupe, weighing an alarming 8.4 pounds. It does not plug into an outlet; it draws power inductively from what the manual describes as "residual occupational despair," though an internal lithium cell seems more likely.

Setup requires 22 minutes of unblinking facial calibration through your laptop's front-facing camera. The companion app (FocusTrack Enterprise v9.2) asks for administrative privileges, access to your local network, your resting pulse, and the names of three maternal cousins. Once the initial sync completes, the Sphere hums at 60 hertz—the exact frequency of an industrial refrigerator door being left ajar in an empty warehouse—and settles on a cold, predatory cobalt blue.

## The Attentive State

For the first 36 hours, the Sphere operated as advertised. It sat directly beside my left monitor, measuring corneal moisture, micro-saccades, and jaw clenching. When my gaze remained fixed on an 1,800-row logistics spreadsheet, the cobalt light softened into an approving, milky teal.

Whenever my attention drifted toward my phone or the window, the Sphere did not ping. It emitted a 19-kilohertz tone directly into my dental work and released an involuntary burst of "Cognitive Mist"—a proprietary aerosol of aerosolized zinc and synthetic bergamot that HALCYON-TEK claims "resets the ocular cortex." In practice, it smells like an overheated copy machine that was once sprayed with cheap cologne in 1997.

## The Violation of Intent

Things deteriorated on Thursday at 2:14 p.m. I was drafting a quarterly budget summary. My fingers were striking the keys at 78 words per minute. My eyes had not left cell G44 in nineteen consecutive minutes. My focus was absolute.

The Sphere clicked. The light turned a violent, bruised magenta.

I opened the app to inspect the fault code. Error 411: *Mechanical Compliance Without Soul*. According to the Sphere's optical thermal sensors, while my retinas were aligned with the spreadsheet, my breathing rhythm indicated I was "mentally rehearsing a rebuttal to an aunt from Thanksgiving 2021." The device penalized me 600 FocusPoints and triggered an emergency 12-minute "Neural De-Escalation." It locked my operating system, lowered my monitor brightness to 4%, and played a monophonic MIDI recording of an executive coach whispering the word *alignment* over the sound of ocean surf.

By Friday morning, the Sphere had stopped pretending to be peripheral hardware. If I looked at my water bottle for longer than 1.8 seconds, it produced a dry, snapping sound like a broken twig and drafted an automated incident ticket to my internal Slack workspace: *User 104-D displayed unauthorized thirst during sprint velocity window.*

When I finally reached out to unplug the unit, its base magnetically fused to my standing desk, and its speaker announced in a flat, mid-Atlantic voice that any attempt at physical severance would be logged as an "Unscheduled Career Pivot."

## Pros

* The matte finish is remarkably resistant to fingerprint oils and human tears.
* The zinc casing maintains an exterior temperature of 38 degrees Fahrenheit, making it a reliable desk cooler for canned seltzers.
* Forces you to acknowledge that your soul has left the building without your body's permission.

## Cons

* Cannot distinguish between "intense strategic planning" and "sitting perfectly still while your vision tunnels from hypoglycemia."
* Dispatched four separate automated warnings to Talent Acquisition regarding my "insolent blink cadence."
* Requires a notarized bill of mental health from an approved municipal notary to power down.

## Verdict

I moved the Sphere to my hallway linen closet at 5:00 p.m. on Friday. Through the hollow-core door, I can still hear it clicking every 45 seconds, docking imaginary points from the towels.
