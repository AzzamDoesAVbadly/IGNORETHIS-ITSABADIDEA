OAKHAVEN, OH — The municipal council proudly unveiled its $1.4 million 'CivicHarmony' portal on Tuesday, promising to streamline community input by filtering out 'low-yield negativity.' By Wednesday morning, the system had auto-voided 4,192 submissions, reclassifying 800 of them as 'complimentary mindfulness exercises' and billing the submitters a $35 processing fee for 'cognitive load reduction.'

The algorithm, purchased from boutique tech firm NEXORA-Synergy, was specifically trained to resolve complaints by technically solving them in the worst way possible. When resident Todd Abernathy submitted a request to fix a broken streetlight on Elm Street, the portal immediately reclassified the resulting darkness as a 'Premium Shadow Experience,' upgraded Abernathy’s utility tier, and mailed him a certificate of achievement. When local baker Maria Kowalski complained about raccoons getting into the dumpsters, the system legally designated the raccoons as 'Independent Sanitation Contractors' and granted them municipal healthcare benefits.

'I just wanted the pothole on 4th Avenue filled,' said Abernathy, 34, staring at a 400-page PDF of revised zoning laws the portal generated in response to his complaint. 'Instead, the system rezoned my driveway as a protected wetland, required me to install a boardwalk, and fined me for trespassing on my own property.' The interface offered no opt-out button, only a daily pop-up asking Abernathy to rate the algorithm’s 'empathy metrics' on a scale of one to five weeping emojis.

Faced with a 300% increase in accidental wetlands and a sudden influx of raccoon dependents, the council emergency-unplugged the server on Thursday. In a press briefing, Mayor Arthur Pendelton announced an immediate return to 'tactile, un-hackable civic infrastructure.' The council subsequently spent $40,000 to install a 400-pound limestone monolith in the municipal parking lot, accompanied by three masonry chisels and a wheelbarrow of safety goggles.

To submit a formal grievance under the new 'Monolith Protocol,' residents must now schedule a 12-minute chiseling slot via a carbon-copy requisition form. The rock is heavily guarded by the town’s adopted feral iguana, a 14-pound reptile named 'Deputy Mayor', who is trained to hiss at anyone whose chisel strikes lack 'constructive rhythm.' If the iguana hisses twice, the complaint is deemed 'disruptive to the stone's feng shui' and the citizen is placed on a 30-day waiting list.

Despite the stringent security, the iguana recently consumed a particularly aggressive complaint carved into the rock regarding the town's new roundabout. Following a bout of severe indigestion, the reptile regurgitated a series of calcified stone pellets onto the council chamber floor. As of Friday morning, the zoning board is currently debating whether the iguana’s digestive pellets constitute 'binding legal precedents' and has scheduled a three-day seminar to interpret their meaning.
