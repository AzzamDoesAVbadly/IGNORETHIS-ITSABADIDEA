OAKHAVEN, OH — At 3:42 p.m. Saturday, the synth-pop trio Pixel Purgatoria commenced a four-hour performance entirely contained within a refurbished snack dispenser. The group’s manager, a woman known only as 'The Operator,' confirmed that the band's audio feed is hard-wired into the machine's coin-op trigger, meaning songs only progress once a specific SKU has been successfully dispensed.

## The Technical Rig

Pixel Purgatoria—vocalist Maya Lichten, guitarist Eli Trotter, and drummer Samir “Splice” Ghosh—spent three weeks soldering a custom MIDI controller into the machine’s internal cooling fan. The resulting sound is a high-frequency hum that oscillates based on the machine's internal temperature, which the band calls 'the thermal heartbeat of capitalism.' 

“We found that the song ‘Static Drift’ really only hits its emotional peak when the compressor kicks in to keep the granola bars at 42 degrees,” Lichten said, while wearing a headset made of recycled soda tabs. “If the crowd doesn't buy enough pretzels, we literally cannot reach the chorus. It creates a high-stakes environment where the art is dependent on a craving for salted nuts.”

## The User Experience

Attendees were required to wear biometric wristbands that synced with the machine’s inventory. As the set progressed, the band increased the 'audio cost' of their tracks. A simple verse could be unlocked with a 99-cent bag of corn chips, but the climactic drum solo required a simultaneous purchase of a large sparkling water and a pack of peppermint patties.

“I spent $14.50 just to hear the second half of the bridge,” said local resident Gary Finch, 51, who was seen clutching three bags of honey-roasted peanuts. “It was an agonizing experience, but when the beat finally dropped and a bag of beef jerky hit the tray at 110 miles per hour, I felt a spiritual connection to the music that I’ve never experienced with a Spotify playlist.”

## Mechanical Failure

At 4:15 p.m., the performance hit a critical snag when the machine’s spiral arm jammed on a package of toasted coconut squares, effectively trapping the band’s bass line in a permanent, distorted loop. Vending technician Tommy Rusk, 34, was called to the scene but refused to intervene, citing the 'artistic integrity of the jam.'

“I told them the motor wasn't rated for a 120-bpm tempo,” Rusk said, leaning against a trash can. “But they insisted on syncing the snare hits to the vending cycle. Now the machine thinks it’s out of stock, but it’s actually just playing a very aggressive B-flat.”

## The Escalation

By 5:00 p.m., the performance reached its zenith when the band announced a 'Premium Tier' finale. To hear the final track, 'Vacuum-Sealed Heart,' the audience had to pool their money to purchase every remaining item in the machine. As the final bag of pretzels dropped, the machine suffered a catastrophic electrical surge, triggering the mall's industrial fire suppression system.

As the food court was drenched in 400 gallons of chemical retardant, the machine began emitting a piercing, 110-decibel screech that The Operator described as 'the sound of a dying retail ecosystem.' The performance concluded when the vending machine exploded, showering the drenched crowd in a rain of lukewarm powdered donuts and shattered glass, while the digital display flickered one last time to read: PLEASE INSERT EXACT CHANGE.
