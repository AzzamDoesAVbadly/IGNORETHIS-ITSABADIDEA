SEATTLE, WA — A 3‑year‑old ficus known online as “Fernanda the Foliage” began fielding relationship questions Tuesday, turning a corner office plant into the city’s first botanical advice columnist. The plant, which occupies a 12‑inch pot on the 27th floor of the NEXORA‑Tech hub, now replies to 14 daily emails with advice that reads like a self‑help book written in chlorophyll.

"I’m just trying to grow," Fernanda’s caretaker, Maya Delgado, 29, senior UX designer, told reporters. "When someone asked how to keep a long‑distance romance alive, I suggested a 30‑minute sunlight window at 9 a.m., because consistency is the soil of trust."

## The first letters

The column, titled *Leaf‑Level Love*, opened with a query from 31‑year‑old freelance illustrator Jonah Patel, who asked how to tell his partner he was “ready for a commitment, but not ready to commit to a houseplant.” Fernanda responded with a three‑step protocol: 1) schedule weekly waterings at 3 p.m.; 2) measure ambient humidity with a hygrometer and maintain 65 % ± 2 %; 3) place a small rock in the pot to symbolize emotional stability. "If you can’t remember to water me on Tuesdays, you’ll never remember an anniversary," the response read.

Dr. Lila Moss, 42, behavioral ecologist at the Institute for Plant‑Human Interaction, called the advice “a perfect example of cross‑species empathy engineering.” She added that “plants process stress signals via volatile organic compounds, so when you neglect a ficus, your own cortisol spikes by an average of 7.3 %.”

## The growth plan

Fernanda’s column quickly attracted a steady stream of queries. A 27‑year‑old barista named Carlos Vega asked how to break up with a roommate who kept stealing his reusable straw. Fernanda suggested a “pruning ceremony” where both parties trim a leaf together, then re‑pot the cuttings in separate containers as a visual metaphor for independence. The advice was accompanied by a diagram of a leaf split down the midrib, annotated in Verdana‑12.

In another exchange, 45‑year‑old senior accountant Priya Singh wondered whether “ghosting” was acceptable after a three‑month dating trial. Fernanda replied, “Ghosting is akin to dropping a plant without warning; the roots suffer, and the soil becomes barren. Instead, provide a clear end‑of‑season notice—preferably in writing, with a date and a recommended watering schedule for the next plant you’ll adopt.”

The column also features a “soil‑check” checklist that readers can print on recycled paper. Items include: 1) water volume last week (average 3.2 L), 2) sunlight exposure minutes per day (target 45 min), 3) humidity variance (no more than ±5 % from 65 %). The checklist ends with a reminder: “If you’re consistently under‑watering, you may be emotionally under‑committing.”

## The inevitable pruning

The popularity of Fernanda’s advice has led to an unexpected side effect: a surge in pot‑size upgrades. According to NEXORA‑Tech’s facilities manager, the plant now occupies a 20‑inch ceramic pot with a built‑in moisture sensor that triggers a Slack notification when levels dip below 40 %.

“People are treating their relationships like they treat their succulents—over‑watering, under‑watering, and occasionally repotting them in a panic,” said Maya Delgado, who now receives a daily “relationship health” report alongside her sprint burndown chart.

The column’s next edition promises a special on “photosynthetic flirting,” which will advise readers to position themselves near a window at 10 a.m. to maximize chlorophyll‑derived confidence.

The final piece of advice, printed in bold green font at the bottom of the email, warns: "If you ignore the humidity gauge for more than 48 hours, we will file a formal complaint with the Department of Plant‑Based Relationships and you will be permanently blacklisted from all future leaf‑level counsel."
