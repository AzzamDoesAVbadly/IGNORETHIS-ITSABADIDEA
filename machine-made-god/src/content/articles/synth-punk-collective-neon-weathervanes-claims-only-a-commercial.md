FREMONT, CA — Neon Weathervanes, a synth-punk collective known for their refusal to use any instrument manufactured after 1984, held a three-hour livestream Tuesday from 'The Suds Stop,' a 24-hour coin-op laundromat. The band claims that the natural harmonic resonance of a Maytag industrial dryer is the only way to achieve a sound they describe as 'chemically aggressive.'

Lead vocalist Jax Marlow, 27, who wore a jumpsuit made entirely of fused dryer sheets, spent forty minutes of the set explaining that digital plugins are 'cowardly' compared to the raw, physical violence of a spin cycle. “A plugin simulates a vacuum,” Marlow shouted over a 110-decibel hum. “But a Speed Queen 3000 provides an actual, tactile displacement of air that vibrates the calcium in your teeth. That’s where the art happens.”

To facilitate the performance, the band rigged a series of contact microphones to the exterior of four front-loading washers, routing the vibration through a series of pedals that cost more than the laundromat’s real estate value. The set peaked during the song 'Bleach Burn,' where the rhythm section was replaced entirely by the sound of a stray sneaker tumbling in Dryer No. 7.

“It’s a very specific frequency,” said Dr. Silas Vane, 51, a Senior Fellow at the Institute for Unnecessary Sonic Experiments. “We’ve found that a load of 100% Egyptian cotton produces a warm, buttery mid-range, whereas a load of synthetic gym clothes creates a jagged, industrial shriek that can actually cause mild vertigo in people with inner-ear imbalances. It’s a sonic disaster, and therefore, it's genius.”

The group has since launched a tiered subscription model for their upcoming ‘Wash-Day Sessions.’ The basic tier offers a low-bitrate stream, while the ‘Permanent Press’ tier ($29/month) allows users to remotely trigger a high-heat cycle via an app. The most expensive tier, ‘The Heavy Duty,’ costs $115 per month and includes a monthly shipment of the actual lint collected from the band's amplifiers, vacuum-sealed in medical-grade plastic.

As the night concluded, the band attempted to achieve what Marlow calls ‘The Absolute Zero’—a state of total sonic purity reached only when a machine reaches maximum RPM and then abruptly stops. The attempt resulted in the catastrophic failure of the laundromat’s circuit breaker, plunging the block into darkness and leaving three customers’ delicates permanently fused to the drum of Washer 12.
