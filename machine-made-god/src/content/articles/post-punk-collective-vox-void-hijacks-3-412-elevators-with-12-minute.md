ROCKFORD, IL — Six‑piece post‑punk outfit Vox Void launched *Elevated Static* on Tuesday by pushing a proprietary firmware update to exactly 3,412 elevator units in mid‑tier office towers across the Midwest. Each of the album’s 12 tracks runs for 3 minutes 42 seconds, matching the average 5.23‑second dwell time between floors 2 and 7 in a standard 12‑story building.

The band formed in 2022 after a chance encounter at a DIY synth workshop in the basement of a local hardware store, where they repurposed three salvaged fluorescent tubes and a broken vending‑machine coin chute into a “industrial‑soul” instrument. Their mission, as printed on the patch’s release notes, is “to make music that literally lifts people up… and never lets them down.”

"We wanted a listening experience that couldn’t be paused, skipped, or turned down," said lead vocalist Mara Liddell, 27, while standing beside a refurbished 1984‑era elevator button panel (model EB‑9, LED‑green) during a press event held in the lobby of the former Rockford Mall’s vacant food court. "If someone tries to mute it, the elevator just stops. That's the point."

## Release rollout

Vox Void partnered with AscendAudio, a startup that installed a custom firmware patch on the 3,412 elevators. The patch streams the album at a steady 78 dB, loud enough to drown out the typical 42 dB hum of motor bearings but calibrated to stay under the 85 dB fire‑alarm trigger threshold. Every time a rider presses the “door open” button, the system logs the event and updates a real‑time “crowd‑density meter” on the building’s lobby monitor, which now displays a rotating wheel of “existential angst” versus “mundane compliance.”

The patch also locks the doors for the duration of each chorus, forcing passengers to listen to the full lyrical segment before the elevator can resume its ascent. In response, AscendAudio chief engineer Marco “Gear” Alvarez, 31, noted that the firmware now includes a fallback where, if a rider attempts to force‑open the doors, the elevator plays a 30‑second “apology” jingle composed entirely of elevator‑ding sounds.

## Building response

Tenants have reacted in ways that range from bewildered to oddly grateful. "I was on a three‑minute call with my mother when the elevator launched into a chorus about broken fluorescent lights," complained Janine Ortiz, 44, senior accountant at a regional insurance firm. "My boss thinks it’s a new mindfulness exercise and scheduled a ‘soul‑sync’ meeting for next Thursday."

Facilities manager Toby Kline, 34, reported a 27 % drop in complaints about rogue music playlists after the rollout. "Before, we had to police personal Bluetooth speakers. Now the only thing we have to police is whether the chorus is too uplifting for compliance training," he said, gesturing toward a spreadsheet titled “Elevator Engagement Metrics – Q3.”

Fire marshal Denise Harlow, 52, filed a “noise variance exemption” after the building’s alarm system was reprogrammed to play a soft synth pad during the album’s bridge, ensuring that the alarm’s 92 dB siren never competes with the band’s third‑track synth line.

## Next sonic frontier

Buoyed by the elevator rollout, Vox Void announced a follow‑up release that will broadcast exclusively through the public address systems of municipal water‑treatment plants, citing the “soothing cadence of pump motors” as the ideal backdrop for their upcoming concept album, *Hydro‑Hymns*.

The band’s manager, Eli Turner, 29, revealed plans to embed a 7‑minute drone into the control room of the Glenhaven Nuclear Reactor, where the music will be paired with the plant’s coolant‑pump rhythm. "If the reactor’s turbines can hum along, we’ll have achieved true omnipresent relevance," Turner said, while holding a schematic that listed the reactor’s “vibrational harmony index” at 0.86.

The firmware patch is slated for an additional 58 office towers by month‑end, meaning commuters across the region will soon have no escape from the elevator’s enforced soundtrack—unless they choose to take the stairs, in which case they’ll miss the final chorus entirely.
